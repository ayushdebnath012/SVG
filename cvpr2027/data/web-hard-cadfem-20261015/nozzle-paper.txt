Development of Design‐Phase Digital Twin for Structural Optimization of a Biomedical Device

Digital twins have transformed design and verification workflows in engineering, yet their adoption within medical device development remains limited. Conventional build‐and‐test approaches restrict design space exploration and often delay identification of mechanically critical risks. This paper presents a case study on the development of a calibrated, design‐phase behavioral digital twin for a capsule‐based biomedical delivery system used in dentistry, with the annular snap‐fit nozzle‐to‐body interface selected as a representative mechanically sensitive feature. Empirical testing, analytical snap‐fit theory and nonlinear Finite Element Analysis (FEA) were integrated to characterize mating force, strain and structural safety during assembly. Strong pre‐calibration agreement was observed between the analytical and numerical models and back‐calculation of an effective polymer‐to‐polymer coefficient of friction ( μ  = 0.12) reduced prediction error relative to empirical measurements to within experimental repeatability, establishing a validated behavioral digital twin. Design optimization employed a Taguchi orthogonal array to efficiently explore strain‐constrained geometric variation. Results identified undercut depth, governed by nozzle inner diameter, as the dominant contributor to retention strength. An optimized configuration achieved a 65%–75% increase in mating force relative to the baseline design while remaining within permissible strain limits. Numerical verification predicted peak mating force of 190 N and identified localized stress concentrations in the nozzle neck region, highlighting reinforcement requirements before production tooling. Physical verification and validation of the optimized system confirmed the predicted increase in structural performance. Consistent with the digital twin maturity continuum, the framework is positioned as a design‐phase, behavioral digital twin. It operates with offline one‐way physical‐to‐digital data flow during the product realization stage, with automated bidirectional synchronization identified as the next stage of development. These findings demonstrate how digital twins can shift biomedical device development from reactive validation towards predictive, manufacturability‐aware design, strengthening patient safety, clinician confidence and regulatory assurance in Class II medical devices.

A digital twin optimized the structural performance of a multi‐component biomedical device interface by 75%. The work shows that wider adoption of digital twins can accelerate safer, more predictive medical device development across design, verification, and optimization stages.
 


Capsule‐based delivery systems are biomedical devices routinely used in dentistry, including orthodontics, to store, mix, and dispense medical‐grade materials such as glass ionomer cements, composites, and bone cements [ 1 ]. Reliable structural performance in these systems is clinically important because system failure can compromise storage, mixing, and dispensing, with subsequent patient safety risks. These failure modes are challenging to control consistently in multi‐component systems because structural integrity is influenced by multiple interacting factors; hence standardized mechanical benchmarks for structural integrity in modern capsule‐based delivery systems remain limited. The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 20749:2023 provides requirements for pre‐capsulated amalgam systems, including dimensional tolerances and mass loss; however, current capsule‐based biomedical delivery systems used with modern medical‐grade materials lack comparable integrity criteria [ 2 ]. Variation in multi‐component capsule joining strategies across manufacturers further reinforces this gap, with reported architectures including ultrasonic‐welded interfaces, annular snap‐fit, and cantilever snap‐fit mechanisms [ 3 ]. Each joining strategy carries different trade‐offs between retention strength, manufacturability, and mechanical compliance, while the biomedical engineering expectation remains the same: the biomedical device must withstand high‐frequency mixing and resist the high detachment forces generated during medical‐grade material extrusion without leakage or mechanical compromise.

Although commercial capsule‐based biomedical delivery systems differ in geometry, assembly strategies, and branding, their functional architecture is broadly consistent across the industry. Each system must satisfy four essential requirements:
 Storage of two phases of medical‐grade material, typically a liquid and a powder, in separate enclosed chambers to ensure shelf stability. High‐frequency mixing of the liquid and powder components to create a homogenous medical‐grade material within an enclosed chamber. Delivery of the homogenously mixed medical‐grade material through controlled extrusion via an orifice to the intended clinical site.

To meet these functional needs within a single‐use, reliable, safe and cost‐efficient format that complies with Class II medical device regulations, the industry has converged on a modular design/architecture composed of four primary plastic components, each serving a distinct mechanical and functional role. These components are commonly referred to in the industry as:
 
 The plunger : a piston‐like element that travels axially within the capsule body, compressing the mixed medical‐grade material and generating hydraulic pressure. 
 The sachet : an internal bucket, typically incorporating a diaphragm that separates the liquid and powder. 
 The body : the main chamber and structural foundation to which all other components interface. 
 The nozzle : a tapered orifice that directs the medical‐grade material to the target site.

These four components must integrate reliably to provide a consistent delivery system (Figure  1 ). However, the interface between the body and the nozzle remains the most mechanically vulnerable region. During high‐force extrusion, substantial internal hydraulic pressures can develop, particularly when dispensing highly viscous medical‐grade materials. If the nozzle‐body interface lacks sufficient structural integrity, whether due to poor mechanical fit, material creep, or fatigue, nozzle dismount may occur, compromising the procedure, introducing patient safety risks, and increasing clinical waste.

Conventional medical device development often relies on iterative build and test workflows, where mechanically critical risks may only become evident after tooling changes or late‐stage validation [ 4 ,  5 ,  6 ,  7 ,  8 ]. Predictive digital workflows offer an alternative pathway that supports design control earlier in development, potentially strengthening translational evidence for Class II medical devices operating under ISO governed quality and risk management systems [ 9 ,  10 ]. Digital twins extend this concept by integrating physical testing with analytical and computational models to create a calibrated virtual representation of device behavior, enabling preclinical decision making before committing to tooling modification or large prototype programs.

Finite Element Analysis (FEA) is a mature methodology within dental biomechanics, supporting research and preclinical evaluation across implantology, prosthodontics, orthodontics, and trauma simulation [ 11 ,  12 ,  13 ,  14 ,  15 ,  16 ]. Despite this maturity, validated structural modelling of capsule joint integrity, particularly for annular snap‐fit nozzle retention, remains underreported. Annular snap‐fit joints have an established analytical foundation in polymer engineering and are widely used in restorative capsules, including designs where the joint is intended to be inseparable once assembled during manufacture [ 3 ]. Retention behavior is governed primarily by geometric undercut and contact conditions, including friction, yet typical modelling assumptions often adopt generic friction coefficients that may not reflect as molded polymer interfaces. This creates a translational gap between design intent and evidence, where joint performance remains difficult to predict across manufacturing variation and clinical loading.

The present study addresses this gap through the development and validation of a calibrated structural digital twin for an annular snap‐fit nozzle‐to‐body interface in a capsule‐based biomedical delivery system. Empirical mating force testing of a representative commercial capsule design was integrated with a published analytical snap‐fit formulation and nonlinear FEA to characterize assembly mechanics, strain, and structural safety margins. An effective polymer‐to‐polymer coefficient of friction was then back calculated from empirical results to calibrate the digital twin and improve predictive fidelity. Taguchi L9 design screening was used to explore strain constrained geometric variation and identify retention dominant parameters under permissible short‐term strain and Factor of Safety (FoS) constraints aligned with polymer snap‐fit guidance and ISO oriented design assurance principles [ 3 ,  9 ,  17 ]. Physical validation of the optimized system was completed through additive manufacture of nozzles followed by Instron testing under matched assembly conditions, closing the loop between as designed, as simulated, as built, and as tested states.

To position this contribution, the framework is described using the digital twin concepts and terminology established in ISO/IEC 30173:2023, which defines a digital twin as the digital representation of a target entity with data connections that enable convergence between physical and digital states at an appropriate rate of synchronization [ 18 ]. Within the widely adopted Kritzinger et al. taxonomy, digital models, digital shadows and digital twin maturity are distinguished by the direction and automation of data flow: a digital model exchanges data manually in both directions, a digital shadow adds an automated one‐way physical‐to‐digital flow, and a digital twin requires automated bidirectional exchange [ 19 ]. Under these criteria the offline implementation reported here corresponds to the digital‐model stage, since empirical calibration data and design‐parameter updates are exchanged manually rather than through automated links. It is therefore presented as a calibrated, validated behavioral representation that forms the design‐phase foundation for a digital twin, explicitly staged for maturation toward digital‐shadow and digital‐twin integration [ 18 ,  19 ].

Recent reviews of digital twins in medical and healthcare device development converge on a consistent gap. There is a shortage of practical implementations demonstrated through detailed case studies and real‐world applications that establish feasibility and clinical impact, a particular absence of applications and frameworks at the device level, and a need for rigorous validation studies accompanied by transparent reporting of results, including inconclusive findings [ 5 ,  20 ,  21 ]. The contribution of the present work is therefore not the individual analytical, numerical, or optimization methods, but their calibrated and empirically validated integration applied to a specific and underreported problem, directly addressing the case‐study, device‐level, and validation gaps these reviews identified.

On this basis, the specific contributions of this work are as follows: To our knowledge, the first validated structural characterization of annular snap‐fit nozzle retention in a capsule‐based biomedical delivery system, integrating empirical testing, analytical snap‐fit theory, and nonlinear FEA in a single calibrated, multi‐layer workflow that predicts both assembly mating force and structural safety. An effective polymer‐to‐polymer coefficient of friction ( μ  = 0.12), back‐calculated against empirical data, that reconciles published snap‐fit theory with as‐molded device behavior and is directly reusable in Finite Element Method (FEM) modeling of polymer mating surfaces. A manufacturability‐aware in silico optimization that identifies undercut depth, governed by nozzle inner diameter, as the dominant retention driver and achieves a 65%–75% improvement in structural performance through a low‐complexity tooling change. A transferable route toward more standardized assessment of mechanically critical interfaces in capsule‐based biomedical delivery systems.

A structured digital twin framework informed by Model Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) principles was used to integrate analytical, numerical, and empirical modelling layers, with full framework description provided in the Figure  S1  [ 22 ].

The data architecture of the framework and its principal elements are mapped to the terminology defined in ISO/IEC 30173:2023 [ 18 ]. The target entity is the annular snap‐fit nozzle‐to‐body interface of the capsule‐based biomedical delivery system, together with the assembly process by which the nozzle is engaged with the body. The corresponding digital representation comprises three coupled behavioral layers, namely an analytical snap‐fit model, a nonlinear FEM model and an empirical test dataset, integrated within an MBSE structure [ 22 ]. The three layers were coordinated through a system model developed in CATIA Magic Cyber Systems Engineer 2024×, which acted as the central integration layer: each layer was bound as a distinct behavioral element to the nozzle‐to‐body retention requirement and exchanged data with the model offline, through controlled manual inputs rather than direct model‐to‐model coupling.

The physical‐to‐digital data connection comprises empirical force‐displacement traces obtained from Instron mating‐force testing, together with dimensional inspection of as‐molded specimens, which are used to calibrate and validate the digital representation. The peak mating force extracted from each Instron force‐displacement trace is recorded in the system model as the empirically derived value of the mating‐force. This recorded value provides the reference against which the analytical and FEM model mating‐force predictions are compared within the model. The force‐displacement data therefore enter the MBSE structure as a discrete, manually transferred verification input rather than through live instrumentation [ 22 ]. The digital‐to‐physical data connection comprises design‐parameter updates identified through optimization, which are fed back to the manufacturing definition as discrete geometric tooling changes. In the present implementation, both connections are executed offline and manually rather than through automated instrumentation, so the synchronization rate is discrete and event‐based, aligned with design and product‐realization milestones rather than continuous real‐time or near real‐time operation [ 18 ].

A focused review of patented capsule‐based biomedical delivery systems was conducted to contextualize nozzle retention strategies used across manufacturers (Figure  2 ). Three primary architectures were identified: ultrasonic welded joints, annular snap‐fits, and cantilever snap‐fits. Welded interfaces provide high retention but limited compliance, while cantilever designs introduce local flexibility at the expense of geometric complexity. Annular snap‐fit joints, used by multiple manufacturers, rely on controlled radial interference to generate circumferential compression and frictional resistance.

Despite differences in joining mechanisms, the patents reveal a largely consistent internal capsule architecture across manufacturers.

The annular snap‐fit architecture was selected for detailed investigation because it is widely used and mechanically sensitive to small geometric and interfacial variations. Welded and cantilever designs were included for contextual comparison only.

At present, no internationally recognized standard exists for quantifying nozzle mating or retention behavior in capsule‐based biomedical delivery systems. Consequently, the empirical testing protocol was not conducted under a specific ISO test standard or certified quality management procedure.

A single representative annular snap‐fit capsule design (SDI) was tested. Nineteen legacy specimens were conditioned at 21°C for 4 h. Tests were performed using an Instron 5965 electromechanical testing system fitted with a 500 N tension‐compression transducer and Bluehill Universal v4.38 software. At the time of testing, the Instron system was within its current calibration interval and held a valid ISO 7500‐1:2018 Class 0.5 verification over 2%–100% of full‐scale compression [ 23 ]. A custom fixture was used to replicate capsule production alignment. The capsule body was pressed into the nozzle at a constant crosshead speed of 0.75 mm/s and the peak mating force at full engagement was recorded. The empirical mean peak force was used as the reference for assessing model agreement and for calibration.

Analytical predictions were derived from Bayer's annular snap‐fit model for polymer joints [ 3 ], adapted to the biomedical device context where the nozzle provides the primary elastic compliance and the body behaves comparatively rigid. For the present configuration, the body was treated as a rigid shaft and the nozzle as an elastic hub. The corresponding geometric factor,  X N , was calculated using Bayer's geometry‐dependent expression (Equation  1 ) [ 3 ]: 
 
 (1) 
 X N = 0.62 · d d i − 1 / d d i + 1 d d i 2 + 1 / d d i 2 − 1 − v 
 
where 0.62 is the configuration constant for a rigid shaft and elastic hub arrangement,  d i  is the inside diameter of the shaft at the snap bead,  d  is the outer diameter of the shaft at the snap bead and  v  is Poisson's ratio. A value of  v  = 0.45 was used in this study as a generic value for Polypropylene (PP).

The transverse force  P  for an annular snap‐fit near the end of the tube was then approximated from (Equation  2 ) [ 3 ]: 
 
 (2) 
 P = y · d · E s · X N 
 
where  y  is the radial undercut depth,  d  is the joint diameter,  E s  is the secant modulus used to account for the nonlinear behavior of the polymer and  X N  is the geometry factor. In the present study,  E s  = 1 GPa was used as the secant modulus for PP.

The axial mating force W was calculated as a function of transverse deflection force  P , lead angle  α  and coefficient of friction  μ  (Equation  3 ) [ 3 ]:
 
 (3) 
 W = P ( μ + tan α ) 1 − μ tan α 
 
Strain was estimated using Bayer's expression (Equation  4 ) [ 3 ]: 
 
 (4) 
 ϵ = y d · 100 % 
 
These analytical relations were used to generate baseline mating force predictions and to support friction back calculation during digital twin calibration.

A three‐dimensional CAD representation of the nozzle and body interface was created, capturing the annular undercut and insertion path. Nonlinear FEA simulations were performed in SimScale (v2025‐Oct), with geometric nonlinearity enabled to represent the large‐deformation contact behavior associated with snap‐fit engagement during assembly.

The PP nozzle was modelled using a bilinear elasto‐plastic model to capture localized yielding during assembly. The capsule body was modelled as an isotropic linear elastic Polycyclohexylenedimethylene Terephthalate Glycol (PCTG) material, reflecting its comparatively higher stiffness (see Table  1 ).

Contact between the nozzle and body was represented using a penalty‐based physical contact formulation with Coulomb friction. All nozzle faces expected to engage with the body during snap‐fit insertion were assigned as the master contact surfaces, while the corresponding body faces were assigned as the slave surfaces. Moderate contact stiffness was applied; fictitious clearance was disabled and a friction penalty coefficient of  1 · 10 5  was used. The baseline pre‐calibration model used a Coulomb friction coefficient of  μ  = 0.20. Contact solution controls included automatic geometry updating, fixed‐point iterations for contact and friction convergence, Newton‐based contact nonlinearity resolution, convergence stabilization and contact smoothing enabled. Boundary conditions were defined to replicate the assembly event. The bottom‐most face of the body was fully constrained using a fixed support condition to represent production‐like support during insertion. A prescribed displacement of  − 5  mm in the global  z ‐direction was applied to the top‐most face of the nozzle, driving the nozzle into the annular snap‐fit feature. The insertion response was solved using a direct MUMPS solver with Newton nonlinear resolution, adaptive convergence criteria, a relative tolerance of  5 · 10 − 5 , an absolute tolerance of  1 · 10 − 4 , tangent prediction, tangent Jacobian updates and a maximum of 35 Newton iterations per increment.

The simulation was advanced using incremental pseudo‐time stepping over a 1 s interval. A maximum time‐step length of 0.05 s and a minimum time‐step length of  1 · 10 − 5  seconds were used, together with four fixed subdivisions and a maximum subdivision depth of three. Automatic boundary condition ramping was enabled to support stable convergence during the highly nonlinear contact event. The computational domain was discretized using the standard meshing algorithm with automatic global sizing at fineness level 5, generating an unstructured three‐dimensional tetrahedral volumetric mesh. Additional local surface refinement was applied to the interacting snap‐fit faces of both the nozzle and body using automatic sizing at fineness level 10, so that the mechanically critical contact region was resolved more accurately. This meshing strategy resulted in 436.4 k cells and 89.3 k nodes. The numerical mating force was obtained from the summed reaction force in the z‐direction on the top‐most face of the nozzle. This force‐displacement response was used as the numerical prediction of mating force for comparison with the analytical model and empirical testing.

Permissible single assembly strain for the snap‐fit bead was defined using Bayer guidance for partially crystalline polymers under brief snap‐fitting events [ 3 ]. A strain limit of 10.8% was adopted to represent 90% of the PP yield strain, acting as the primary acceptance constraint for the retention feature. Structural safety in adjacent nozzle regions was screened using FoS, calculated as (Equation  5 ): 
 
 (5) 
 FoS = Yield Stress von Mises Stress 
 
 FoS  assessment excluded the snap bead itself, which was governed by the strain‐based acceptance criterion and instead targeted the neck region and geometric transitions where secondary stress concentrations can arise during assembly.

Empirical, analytical, and numerical mating force predictions were compared using the empirical mean as the reference. Calibration was performed by back calculating an effective polymer‐to‐polymer coefficient of friction that aligned analytical and numerical predictions with empirical behavior. The calibrated parameters were then applied consistently across all subsequent analyses, establishing a validated behavioral digital twin.

The calibrated digital twin was used to perform in silico parameter optimization of the annular snap‐fit joint. The optimization strategy adopted in this study was based on design parameters and manufacturing constraints. This approach was selected because it enabled targeted improvement of structural performance while preserving a practical pathway to manufacture. The key manufacturing constraint was that optimization should require only minimal change to the existing injection molding tool, with preference given to modifications that could be achieved through changes to the geometry of the core pin. This can reduce resistance to implementation by avoiding major redesign of the molded component or tooling architecture.

The design space was therefore limited to selected geometric parameters of the annular snap‐fit joint that could be influenced through core‐pin‐driven modifications. These included the nozzle  d i  and  d , where  y  was dependent on  d i  [ 17 ]. These parameters were chosen on the assumption that they strongly influence structural performance during assembly. Changes to  d i  were expected to significantly affect the mating force, making these dimensions appropriate candidates for parameter‐based optimization.

A Taguchi L9 orthogonal array was then used to efficiently explore the selected parameter combinations. This method provided a structured way to assess the influence of the chosen design variables while limiting the number of design cases that needed to be evaluated. Analytical predictions were first used as a rapid screening layer to rank the candidate geometries under the strain constraint, after which the best‐performing configuration was assessed using the calibrated FEM model for computational numerical validation. This run‐and‐check workflow enabled efficient exploration of the parameter space while maintaining alignment with both structural acceptance criteria and manufacturing practicality.

Optimized nozzles were additively manufactured using a Formlabs Form 4B system in Tough 1500 V2 resin. Sixteen optimized specimens were tested using the same conditions as the legacy specimens. Peak mating force distributions were compared against digital twin predictions to verify translation from in silico optimization to physical performance.

Uncertainty in the digital twin predictions was assessed as part of the agreement comparison and calibration workflow. This uncertainty assessment followed the FDA credibility framework for computational modeling and simulation in medical device submissions [ 24 ]. Here, Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) estimates the uncertainty in model outputs arising from uncertainty in model inputs and in model form, while Sensitivity Analysis (SA) estimates and ranks the influence of individual inputs on the outputs and thereby identifies the parameters that most warrant control or calibration [ 24 ]. Aleatory and epistemic sources of uncertainty were considered and characterized as:
 Instron mating‐force measurements through the sample standard deviation, coefficient of variation and ±1σ and ±3σ intervals, with the acceptance band defined as the larger of ±1σ or ±5% of the empirical mean to reflect normal manufacturing and assembly variability rather than performance‐limiting discrepancy [ 25 ]. Polymer‐to‐polymer friction coefficient, the dominant unmeasured input, was treated through calibration: an effective μ was back‐calculated from the empirical mean and its uncertainty was bounded by the ±1σ repeatability band of the calibrating measurements, so that the fitted parameter is reported with an associated uncertainty rather than as a single point value. Geometric input uncertainty (nozzle inner diameter, outer diameter, and undercut) was screened across permissible, strain‐constrained variation. Material‐property uncertainty was represented using nominal PP values from the technical data sheet. Model‐form uncertainty was quantified as the residual discrepancy between the analytical snap‐fit formulation, which assumes beam‐type compliance, a comparatively rigid body, and the nonlinear finite element representation.

Sensitivity to the coefficient of friction was evaluated analytically (Equation  3 ), which permits the local elasticity of mating force with respect to μ to be estimated directly from the calibration shift [ 3 ]. Sensitivity to geometry was evaluated through the Taguchi L9 design‐screening procedure described in Section  2.6 , which functions here as a structured screening of input influence rather than as a standalone optimizer, allowing the dominant retention driver to be separated from secondary stiffness drivers using an optimized number of evaluations [ 17 ].

Finally, the calibrated output uncertainties were propagated to the context of use by interpreting the predicted peak assembly strain and FoS relative to their acceptance thresholds, namely the maximum permissible single‐assembly strain and the FoS criterion, including the proximity of each prediction to its threshold and the contribution of model‐form uncertainty. This proximity‐to‐threshold assessment follows the adequacy‐assessment recommendations of the credibility guidance, under which the sufficiency of a model for its context of use is judged from the totality of the credibility evidence together with the margin between predictions and decision thresholds [ 24 ].

Across all nineteen legacy specimens, the mean maximum mating force measured during assembly was 110 N, with a standard deviation of 9 N, corresponding to a coefficient of variation of 8.2%, a ±1σ interval of 101–119 N and a ±3σ interval of 83–137 N. The standard error of the mean was 1.4 N and the associated 95% confidence interval on the mean was 107–113 N. Force displacement curves showed consistent engagement behavior across specimens, with reproducible peak forces occurring prior to snap through.

The analytical model predicted a peak mating force of 139 N for the baseline system. The pre‐calibration FEM model predicted a peak force of 144 N. Analytical and numerical predictions agreed closely in magnitude but exceeded the empirical mean.

Model‐form uncertainty, quantified as the residual discrepancy between the analytical and numerical predictions, was 3% at the calibration point. The coefficient of friction was identified as a first‐order epistemic input. Reducing μ from the generic literature value of 0.20 to the calibrated value of 0.12, a 40% reduction, reduced the predicted mating force by 22%–24%, after which post‐calibration analytical and numerical predictions agreed to within 97% (Figure  3 ). Following calibration, the prediction error relative to the empirical mean was less than 2%, within the ±1σ experimental repeatability band. This confirms that friction, although unmeasured, exerts a controlling influence on the predicted mating force and justifies its treatment by calibration rather than by assumption [ 24 ].

Post‐calibration agreement between empirical, analytical, and numerical mating force predictions following back calculation of the effective coefficient of friction ( μ  = 0.12) used to refine the digital twin.

The calibrated analytical model predicted a peak assembly strain of 7.65% at the snap‐fit bead, below the defined permissible strain limit. Von Mises stress contours showed progressive stress development within the annular snap‐fit engagement region during assembly, with a peak localized stress of 30 MPa at the bead (Figure  4 ). Outside this intended engagement region, stress levels remained comparatively low. Additional stress distributions were observed in the internal cylindrical portion of the body parallel to the snap‐fit bead during peak insertion, with localized stresses of 10 MPa. The corresponding FoS contours remained at or above 2 across most regions, with localized reductions confined to the snap‐fit interface. No unintended regions exhibited yield‐level stresses (Figure  5 ).

Greater stress concentration was observed at the bead, particularly during the early stages of engagement, where larger regions exhibited stresses in the upper range of the contour scale. The legend scale was therefore adjusted to show a comparable stress distribution to the legacy system. FoS distributions for the optimized system remained broadly consistent with the calibrated behavioral model, with most regions exhibiting FoS values between 1.2 and 2 during assembly (Figure  7 ). Localized FoS reductions approaching 0.8 were observed in the nozzle neck region adjacent to the snap‐fit bead and in the internal cylindrical portion of the body parallel to the snap‐fit bead during peak insertion. The snap‐fit bead itself remained governed by strain‐based acceptance criteria and was excluded from FoS assessment. The additively manufactured optimized systems reproduced and exceeded the mating force predicted by the behavioral digital twin, 256 N measured with a broader ±3σ spread of 136 N (standard deviation ≈45 N, coefficient of variation ≈18%) against the 190 N predicted. This confirmed the direction of the predicted improvement but indicated a magnitude offset with its attribution in Section  4.1.1 .

Traditional capsule development within biomedical delivery systems typically relies on iterative build and test cycles, with limited upfront modelling, narrow exploration of design space and late discovery of mechanically critical risks [ 5 ,  6 ,  8 ]. This approach constrains innovation and can result in conservative or legacy design choices that persist despite changing clinical demands. This study demonstrates a validated, design‐phase digital twin framework for capsule‐based biomedical delivery systems, providing a practical method to enhance safety assurance, optimize structural performance, and streamline device development.

The framework integrates analytical snap‐fit theory [ 3 ], calibrated nonlinear FEA, empirical testing and Taguchi‐based screening within a single workflow [ 17 ]. Rather than treating modeling and testing as sequential or isolated activities, these layers are coupled to provide predictive insight into structural integrity under production relevant assembly conditions. In this way, the digital twin links mechanical engineering decisions directly to device performance metrics, enabling informed design transitions prior to physical tooling fabrication.

Following calibration, the digital twin achieved 97% agreement between the analytical and numerical predictions, while the error relative to empirical data was reduced from 30% to within the limits of experimental repeatability. The acceptance band was defined as the larger of ±1σ or ±5% of the empirical mean, reflecting normal manufacturing and assembly variability rather than performance‐limiting discrepancy [ 25 ]. Within this context, the calibrated models satisfy design‐assurance expectations for predictive use during device development [ 24 ]. This level of predictive fidelity enables mechanical retention behavior to be assessed quantitatively before physical prototyping, supporting earlier identification and mitigation of snap‐fit‐related risks. The alignment between analytical, numerical, and empirical evidence strengthens confidence that the digital twin represents as‐manufactured device behavior rather than idealized modelling assumptions.

The credibility of this prediction is further supported by an explicit uncertainty treatment. The available evidence spans two categories of credibility evidence: model‐calibration evidence and bench‐test validation evidence. The uncertainty analysis also addresses the corresponding FDA credibility factors for model inputs, including UQ and SA, as well as model‐form uncertainty and comparator measurement uncertainty [ 24 ]. SA identified friction and undercut depth as the controlling inputs. UQ showed that the calibrated reference response was well determined, with a 95% confidence interval of 107–113 N, that model‐form uncertainty was on the order of 3%–5%, and that the strain‐governed acceptance decision retained a 29% margin after accounting for these uncertainties. Because the model influence is moderate, with predictions used alongside rather than in place of empirical mating‐force testing, this level of demonstrated credibility is commensurate with the associated model risk for the question of interest. The principal residual limitations are the applicability gap between the as‐printed validation specimens and the as‐molded context of use and the use of local rather than global uncertainty propagation, such as Monte Carlo analysis. Both are identified as priorities for future work, with global uncertainty propagation representing the higher‐rigor approach noted in the credibility guidance [ 24 ].

The consequences of prediction error were also considered. Because the calibrated model tends to overestimate rather than underestimate mating force, any residual error would present as a manufactured device performing below the predicted retention level rather than above it. Under the intended deployment, this discrepancy would be detected through routine quality‐control mechanical testing of the manufactured device, because the digital twin is used to support, not replace, physical verification. This places a continuing reliance on quality assurance and quality control during production to confirm that as‐manufactured retention satisfies the specified acceptance criteria.

The additively manufactured validation specimens reproduced and exceeded the predicted increase in retention, confirming both the direction of improvement and exceedance of the target threshold. However, these specimens do not provide direct validation of the absolute mating force expected from as‐molded states. The offset between the measured mean of 256 N and the numerical prediction of 190 N is attributed to material and process differences between the as‐printed Tough 1500 V2 systems and the as‐molded PP systems represented in the model, together with anisotropy effects associated with additive manufacturing. This offset is therefore interpreted as an applicability limitation under the relevance‐to‐context‐of‐use credibility factor, rather than as a deficiency in model form. Confirmatory testing using as‐molded PP specimens is therefore identified as a target for material‐specific recalibration in future work. Additive manufacturing was selected deliberately for this validation because it provided a close and rapidly available proxy for molded performance, using a resin with mechanical behavior sufficiently analogous to the as‐molded PP, while avoiding the substantial lead time and tooling cost associated with molding tool modification. This enabled faster design iteration during early‐stage development.

Taguchi orthogonal array screening enabled efficient exploration of geometry driven retention behavior using a limited number
of analytical evaluations rather than exhaustive physical prototyping [ 17 ]. This approach allowed rapid identification of strain compliant, mechanically robust systems while remaining aligned with manufacturability constraints.

Within the digital twin framework, Taguchi screening functions as a structured design space reduction tool rather than a standalone optimization method. Coupled with analytical and numerical modelling, it supports informed narrowing of candidate designs prior to higher fidelity simulation or empirical validation.

Integration of empirical, analytical, and numerical methods within a calibrated digital twin enables earlier and more confident design decisions [ 6 ,  26 ]. In this study, agreement between empirical data and calibrated model predictions provided sufficient confidence to select feasible design modifications without additional prototype iterations.

Reducing decision latency in this way can limit rework, shorten development timelines, and lower change control burden during later stages of device development. This is consistent with the intent of ISO 13485:2016 design control principles by demonstrating that mechanically critical risks can be addressed through predictive modelling rather than reactive validation [ 9 ].

Calibration identified an effective polymer‐to‐polymer coefficient of friction of  μ  = 0.12, lower than commonly assumed generic values [ 3 ]. This reduction is consistent with the influence of molding additives and surface finish in high throughput injection molding [ 3 ].

Incorporating such effects into the digital twin avoids systematic overestimation of mating forces and ensures predictions remain representative of manufactured devices rather than nominal material pairings. This highlights the importance of process‐specific calibration when deploying digital twins across different manufacturing routes.

Optimization within the digital twin was guided by both mechanical performance and practical manufacturability. The selected configuration achieved a substantial increase in mating force through a reduction in nozzle  d i , increasing effective undercut depth while leaving other geometric features unchanged. This modification requires only a core pin change, avoiding rework of cavity geometry or secondary tooling features.

The optimized system achieved a 65%–75% increase in mating force relative to the baseline configuration. Numerical analysis identified localized stress concentrations in the nozzle neck region during peak insertion, highlighting areas where reinforcement may be required. This capability illustrates how digital twins support balanced improvement by identifying secondary risks before committing to production tooling.

Although demonstrated for annular snap‐fit joints in capsule‐based biomedical delivery systems, the digital twin framework presented here is applicable to other mechanically sensitive interfaces within biomedical devices. The structured coupling of analytical modelling, numerical simulation, and empirical calibration provides a generalizable approach to predicting structural behavior under assembly and use‐relevant loading while maintaining traceability to device requirements, modelling assumptions, and verification evidence.

Integration of this framework within broader MBSE environments offers a pathway to improved traceability between requirements, modelling assumptions and verification evidence [ 22 ,  27 ]. Such integration supports scalable adoption of digital twins as practical tools for medical device development rather than isolated modelling exercises [ 6 ].

Further refinement of the framework is still warranted. The present study employed a full three‐dimensional FEM model; however, the rotational symmetry of the nozzle‐body interface suggests that an axisymmetric formulation may be sufficient for future investigations. This would likely reduce computational expense and improve the practicality of the digital twin for rapid design iteration. The coefficient of friction was also inferred through calibration against measured mating force, rather than measured independently. Future studies should determine this parameter experimentally, as modelling simplifications may also contribute to overprediction of mating force. Independent friction characterization would therefore improve confidence in parameter selection and strengthen the physical basis of the model. A complementary direction is to validate the fidelity of the Taguchi ranking itself by fabricating and testing additional candidate geometries from Table  2 . Whereas the present study deliberately carried the single highest mating force, strain‐compliant candidate through to physical validation as the solution to the stated single‐objective design problem, testing further candidates would confirm that the screened analytical ranking is reproduced in manufactured parts and would further support translation of this digital twin methodology to broader medical device innovation contexts.

The representation developed in this study is deliberately scoped as a design‐phase behavioral digital twin rather than a fully operational digital twin. It does not incorporate real‐time interaction, online synchronization, or autonomous adaptive updating during device deployment. Instead, physical‐to‐digital data flow is established offline through empirical calibration and validation, while digital‐to‐physical feedback is implemented through manual updates to design parameters and the manufacturing process.

This scope is appropriate to the translational problem addressed in this study. During product realization, the dominant requirement is to resolve mechanically critical risks and improve structural performance before tooling commitment. At this stage, a calibrated and validated behavioral representation provides decision‐support value without the cost and complexity of live instrumentation. The framework therefore exercises the design‐phase capabilities of a digital twin under ISO/IEC 30173:2023, including connection to empirical data, integration of analytical to numerical models and simulation‐based prediction, while transparently acknowledging that operational synchronization has not yet been implemented [ 18 ].

Progression toward a functional digital twin would require automated, bidirectional and synchronized data flow with deployed devices. Operationally, this would require instrumentation of the physical process and product, for example through in‐line force, displacement and dimensional sensing during assembly and use, with the resulting data streamed to the digital representation through an industrial data infrastructure. Standardized machine‐to‐machine communication protocols and lightweight messaging transports would provide the physical‐to‐digital and digital‐to‐physical channels, while automated model updating and adaptive recalibration would close the loop. Defining, implementing and validating this data infrastructure, including sensor instrumentation, messaging brokers and synchronization logic, is beyond the scope of the present study and represents the principal next stage of this research. The present contribution establishes the calibrated behavioral foundation and requirement traceability onto which such an operational layer can be built.

This study developed and validated a calibrated digital twin framework for predicting and optimizing the structural performance of an interface in capsule‐based biomedical delivery systems. An integrated analytical, numerical, and empirical workflow was used to characterize annular snap‐fit behavior, with calibration aligning the digital and physical domains.

Design‐space screening confirmed undercut depth, governed by nozzle inner diameter, as the dominant driver of mating force. A manufacturability‐constrained improvement achieved a 65%–75% increase in mating force through a single tooling modification, while numerical analysis identified secondary reinforcement requirements before commitment to production‐relevant tooling.

Overall, the study demonstrates how a structured digital twin framework can shift medical device development from reactive validation toward predictive, risk‐informed, and evidence‐informed design. Embedding such workflows within MBSE processes informed by ISO 14971:2019 and ISO 13485:2016 can position digital twins as practical enablers of safer, more efficient, and more evidence‐driven biomedical device innovation [ 9 ,  10 ,  22 ].

The scope of this contribution must also be stated clearly. The digital twin demonstrated here is a calibrated, design‐phase behavioral representation that operates with offline, one‐way physical‐to‐digital data flow and manual digital‐to‐physical feedback during product realization. It is positioned at the design‐phase stage of the digital twin maturity continuum defined in ISO/IEC 30173:2023 and does not provide real‐time, bidirectional synchronization with deployed devices [ 18 ]. Realizing a functional digital twin capable of automated and synchronized bidirectional data exchange with devices in service is the next stage of this work and will require dedicated sensor instrumentation and industrial data infrastructure. The validated behavioral foundation and explicit requirements traceability established in this study provide the technical basis for future development of an operationally synchronized digital twin.
